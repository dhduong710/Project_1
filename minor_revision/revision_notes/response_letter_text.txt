Reviewer #1
This paper introduces SoftFuse-KGC, an evidence-calibration framework designed to improve graph-augmented large language model pipelines for biomedical knowledge graph completion. The framework adds two post-processing modules on top of a lightweight R-GCN-based DrKGC-style backbone: a soft-support reranker that reorders top-20 drug candidates using hand-weighted graph evidence features and penalties for direct shortcuts and contradictory signals, and a confidence-aware fuzzy retrieval module that selects a compact evidence subgraph via greedy edge scoring while preserving candidate ranking. Evaluated across five biomedical datasets, the authors report improvements in candidate mean reciprocal rank on some datasets and reduced graph size after fuzzy compression. Overall, the work is not sufficiently rigorous for publication in its current form and requires major, fundamental revisions.
Comment 1. The related work section fails to clearly differentiate the proposed work from existing evidence reranking and graph compression methods for KGC, and systematically overstates conceptual novelty.
Author response:
We thank the reviewer for highlighting the need for clearer positioning. Related Work now discusses RankRAG, G-Retriever, GRAG, and SubgraphRAG, explaining their approaches to context ranking, compact graph retrieval, and learned triple selection (manuscript pp. 3–4, particularly Section 2.3, p. 4).
We have also revised the novelty claims to emphasize the integration of biomedical evidence cues for candidate reranking and graph selection. This scope is stated in the Abstract (p. 1), the Introduction contributions (p. 3), the Discussion (pp. 17–18), and the Conclusion (pp. 18–19).
Comment 2. All soft-support coefficients and fuzzy-retrieval weights are manually calibrated on validation sets separately for each dataset, with no learnable parameters or automated calibration mechanism. This heavy reliance on dataset-specific hand-tuned hyperparameters severely limits the generalizability of the method to new datasets, tasks, or graph topologies.
Author response:
We thank the reviewer for this concern. We added validation-based coefficient search, a learned scorer using five shared features, and leave-one-dataset-out (LODO) transfer that excludes target calibration labels. Equal-weight controls isolate the effect of learning. For fuzzy retrieval, automatic selection compares 12 coefficient vectors at fixed candidate order and graph budget.
Learned weights improve candidate MRR over the original formula on four datasets and match it on PrimeKG; LODO gives higher point estimates on all five. Against equal weights, the exploratory 95% interval for the learned gain excludes zero only on repoDB. These findings support coefficient reuse within the evaluated benchmarks, with dataset-dependent benefits.
The main findings appear in manuscript Section 4.5 (p. 14) and Table 4 (p. 15). Supplementary Section S12 (pp. 18–23), including Tables S16–S24 (pp. 19–23), provides the fitting, transfer, ablation, and sensitivity analyses.
Comment 3. The authors must provide a quantitative analysis of information retention across edge selection and compare against alternative subgraph selection strategies.
Author response:
We thank the reviewer for this suggestion. Across all five datasets, we compared fuzzy retrieval with random sampling, coverage-based, score-based, breadth-first, PageRank, shortest-path, and relation-stratified selectors, fixing candidate order and the unique-edge budget. The analyses measure candidate coverage, reachability, labeled paths, and encoder representations across multiple budgets.
Fuzzy retrieval preserves incident evidence for all source-covered candidates, while three-hop reachability retention ranges from 0.4835 on Hetionet to 0.9546 on repoDB. It exceeds random selection on PharmKG, DRKG, and repoDB. Shortest-path selection retains more connectivity but covers fewer candidates on some datasets, demonstrating the distinction between candidate coverage and path preservation.
Manuscript Section 4.6 and Table 5 (p. 15) summarize these findings. Supplementary Section S13 (pp. 23–28), Tables S25–S29 (pp. 25–26), and Figs. S4–S6 (pp. 27–28) provide the detailed comparisons.
Comment 4. The proposed method does not improve actual knowledge graph completion performance, it only re-ranks candidates that have already been retrieved by the backbone model.
Author response:
We thank the reviewer for drawing attention to the evaluation scope. SoftFuse-KGC targets candidate ordering and evidence selection within the backbone’s fixed top-20 set. On PrimeKG, candidate MRR@20 increases from 0.0646 to 0.1253 at unchanged Gold@20 of 0.240, while fuzzy retrieval reduces the graph from 59.93 to 32.34 triples. These results support ranking and evidence-efficiency gains at fixed candidate coverage. We clarify this scope in manuscript Section 4.2 (pp. 11–12), with results in Table 3 (p. 13).
During revision, we corrected FP16 overflow affecting 9/500 PrimeKG full-graph test cases and standardized E2E scoring. Re-evaluation used FP32 graph encoding and BF16 inference with unchanged checkpoints and splits and no excluded cases; for example, the source E2E MRR changed from 0.0476 to 0.0477 (manuscript Table 3, p. 13).
We also recalculated validation candidate MRR from candidate identifiers, assigning zero reciprocal rank to absent gold entities. The corrected values appear in manuscript Fig. 2 (p. 8) and Supplementary Section S1.1 (pp. 3–4), Table S6 (p. 11), and Table S13 (p. 15).
Comment 5. Dataset preprocessing and entity alignment procedures are insufficiently documented. Specifically, the cross-resource mapping between UMLS disease identifiers in repoDB and the DRKG gene/disease namespace is not described.
Author response:
We thank the reviewer for identifying this missing detail. We retain repoDB diseases as local UMLS nodes and join DRKG molecular evidence through exact DrugBank compound identifiers, preserving gene identifiers and relations. No disease crosswalk or disease-to-gene conversion is applied. In the prepared DRKG map, 1,130 of 1,571 compounds match; unmatched compounds remain in the task.
Manuscript Section 4.1 (pp. 10–11, repoDB paragraph on p. 11) now describes this alignment. Supplementary Section S11.1 (pp. 17–18) details normalization, deduplication, label conflicts, graph construction, and splitting with held-out target triples excluded. Table S15 (p. 17) distinguishes local graph-ID coverage from cross-resource alignment.
Comment 6. The authors should cite and discuss the following two relevant works to contextualize the proposed method against advanced graph learning paradigms for drug entities, and discuss how more principled multi-source fusion approaches could advance beyond heuristic hand-calibrated evidence scoring: "Spatial-spectral fusion enables drug repositioning by capturing indirect and long-range associations in biological networks",
"FD-MSGL: Drug Repositioning via Frequency-Domain Multi-Source Synergistic Graph Learning".
Author response:
We thank the reviewer for highlighting these relevant studies. Related Work discusses spatial–spectral fusion and FD-MSGL in Section 2.1 (manuscript pp. 3–4); both are included as References 45–46 (p. 23).
The final paragraph of the Discussion (Section 5, p. 18) explains how jointly learning molecular, interaction, and pathway representations with fusion weights could enrich candidate scoring and guide graph selection under edge-budget and path-retention objectives.
