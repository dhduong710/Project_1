We thank the reviewers for their valuable comments. We have revised the manuscript to accommodate or respond to all the points raised. Overall, our revised manuscript has main changes as follows:

1. Expanded Related Work with published evidence-ranking and subgraph-retrieval studies, including the two requested drug-repositioning papers, and narrowed the novelty claims.

Reviewer #1

This paper introduces SoftFuse-KGC, an evidence-calibration framework designed to improve graph-augmented large language model pipelines for biomedical knowledge graph completion. The framework adds two post-processing modules on top of a lightweight R-GCN-based DrKGC-style backbone: a soft-support reranker that reorders top-20 drug candidates using hand-weighted graph evidence features and penalties for direct shortcuts and contradictory signals, and a confidence-aware fuzzy retrieval module that selects a compact evidence subgraph via greedy edge scoring while preserving candidate ranking. Evaluated across five biomedical datasets, the authors report improvements in candidate mean reciprocal rank on some datasets and reduced graph size after fuzzy compression. Overall, the work is not sufficiently rigorous for publication in its current form and requires major, fundamental revisions.

Comment 1. The related work section fails to clearly differentiate the proposed work from existing evidence reranking and graph compression methods for KGC, and systematically overstates conceptual novelty.

Author response:

We thank the reviewer for highlighting the need for clearer positioning. Section 2 now discusses RankRAG (NeurIPS 2024), G-Retriever (NeurIPS 2024), GRAG (Findings of NAACL 2025), and SubgraphRAG (ICLR 2025), distinguishing retrieved-context ranking, compact graph retrieval, and learned triple selection. All four are cited through their published conference versions.

We have narrowed the Abstract, Introduction, Discussion, and Conclusion to describe an application-specific combination of candidate reranking and graph-adapter evidence selection, rather than a new general reranking or graph-compression principle. The revised analyses distinguish candidate coverage from path retention and do not claim lossless compression. Related Work focuses on the prior methods; the contribution and its limits are stated in the Introduction and Discussion.

Revisions: Abstract; Sections 1, 2.1–2.3, 5, and 6.



Comment 2. All soft-support coefficients and fuzzy-retrieval weights are manually calibrated on validation sets separately for each dataset, with no learnable parameters or automated calibration mechanism. This heavy reliance on dataset-specific hand-tuned hyperparameters severely limits the generalizability of the method to new datasets, tasks, or graph topologies.

Author response:

We thank the reviewer for this important concern. We added a five-dataset study comprising validation-only search over 224 original-formula configurations per dataset, a learned linear scorer with five shared structural features, and leave-one-dataset-out (LODO) coefficient transfer. Learning uses a regularized pairwise logistic objective with query-group cross-validation. LODO fitting and hyperparameter selection exclude the target dataset’s calibration labels. Equal-weight controls use the same features, separating the effect of learning from that of changing the representation.

On the complete test splits, shared learned weights improve candidate MRR over the original formula on four datasets and match it on PrimeKG; LODO gives higher point estimates on all five. Against the feature-matched equal-weight control, the repoDB gain is 0.00754, with an exploratory query-cluster 95% interval of [0.00040, 0.01518]; the other four intervals include zero. We therefore report evidence for reusable scoring features without claiming a uniform benefit from learning their coefficients.

For graph selection, we evaluate 12 coefficient vectors per dataset at fixed candidate order and edge budget, with validation-based automatic and source-only selection. Additional analyses cover 20 directed dataset transfers, calibration-label availability, feature ablation, and coefficient sensitivity. Improved candidate ranking does not consistently improve exact generated answers. These experiments address coefficient transfer within prepared benchmark pipelines; they do not establish zero-shot completion on unseen graphs.

Revisions: Section 4.5 and Table 4; Supplementary Section S12, Tables S16–S24; limitations in Section 5.



Comment 3. The authors must provide a quantitative analysis of information retention across edge selection and compare against alternative subgraph selection strategies.

Author response:

We thank the reviewer for requesting a direct information-retention analysis. We now compare fuzzy retrieval with random and coverage-constrained random sampling, native-score and degree selection, breadth-first search, personalized PageRank, shortest-path selection, and relation-stratified selection. Compressed alternatives share the source graph, candidate identities/order, and per-query unique-edge budget. The study covers all five datasets, five random seeds, and budget-sensitivity analyses.

We measure candidate incidence, query–candidate reachability, labeled-path retention, relation distributions, encoder-representation changes, and generated answers. Fuzzy retrieval retains all source-covered candidate incidence, but its three-hop reachability retention is 0.7737/0.9010/0.4835/0.8921/0.9546 on PrimeKG/PharmKG/Hetionet/DRKG/repoDB. It exceeds uniform random selection on PharmKG, DRKG, and repoDB; PrimeKG and Hetionet are similar to random. Shortest-path selection preserves connectivity better but loses incidence coverage on some datasets. Fuzzy selection also removes all eligible length-three witnesses on PharmKG and Hetionet, so preserved incidence is not treated as lossless information retention.

We distinguish duplicate removal from unique-triple removal and report representation changes and exact-answer comparisons separately from adjusted MRR. The downstream comparisons show no universally superior selector. The revised text therefore describes coverage-oriented compression with measured structural trade-offs.

Revisions: Section 4.6 and Table 5; Supplementary Section S13, Tables S25–S29 and Figures S4–S6; Discussion and Conclusion.



Comment 4. The proposed method does not improve actual knowledge graph completion performance, it only re-ranks candidates that have already been retrieved by the backbone model.

Author response:

We thank the reviewer for drawing attention to this distinction. We agree that the method does not expand the backbone’s retrieved candidate set. Its contribution is to rank the available candidates and select the evidence supplied to the graph-augmented generator. We have made this scope explicit: Gold@20 is unchanged, and absent-gold cases cannot be recovered under the candidate-restricted protocol. We do not claim improved full-universe retrieval or discovery of candidates outside that set.

The controlled comparison nevertheless measures a ranking improvement within the fixed retrieval coverage: on PrimeKG, candidate MRR@20 increases from 0.0646 to 0.1253 while Gold@20 remains 0.240. Table 3 additionally evaluates the complete graph–LLM pipeline. Its corrected adjusted E2E MRR rises from 0.0477 to 0.0747, while fuzzy retrieval reduces the mean graph from 59.93 to 32.34 edge occurrences. Because adjusted MRR can credit the gold candidate’s rank even when the generated answer is wrong, this is not evidence of higher exact generation accuracy. Indeed, the standardized PrimeKG exact-match counts are 1/500 for the source and 0/500 for soft support and fuzzy retrieval. Exact-answer results and alternative-selector comparisons are reported separately, and the conclusions have been narrowed accordingly.

Numerical correction. During the additional analyses, we detected FP16 overflow in graph-adapter outputs for nine of the 500 PrimeKG test cases using full source graphs. We repeated the revised module, calibration, and selector evaluations using FP32 graph encoding and BF16 language-model inference, keeping the checkpoints and data splits fixed and excluding no cases. All audited encoder outputs were finite under this policy. Validation-based edge settings were reselected before test evaluation. We also standardized answer matching and adjusted-rank scoring across datasets, whose earlier collectors used differing rules. Thus, the revisions below reflect both the inference correction and scoring harmonization, rather than FP16 overflow alone. We apologize for the earlier inconsistency.

Table R1 reports the previous and revised Table 3 values. On PrimeKG, the source changes from 0.0476 to 0.0477, while both soft-support and fuzzy values remain 0.0747 to four decimals. The corresponding manuscript text and current supplementary comparisons have been synchronized with the corrected results. Earlier generator/decoding diagnostics remain explicitly distinguished in Supplementary Section S7.

Comment 5. Dataset preprocessing and entity alignment procedures are insufficiently documented. Specifically, the cross-resource mapping between UMLS disease identifiers in repoDB and the DRKG gene/disease namespace is not described.

Author response:

We thank the reviewer for identifying this missing detail. Inspection of the preparation code confirmed that no UMLS-to-DRKG disease crosswalk or disease-to-gene identifier mapping is performed. repoDB diseases retain local UMLS IDs; DRKG evidence is joined through exact canonical DrugBank compound IDs, preserving the original gene identifiers and relations. Unmatched compounds remain in the benchmark. We now state this explicitly instead of implying a fully aligned disease namespace.

Supplementary Section S11.1 documents identifier cleaning, pair deduplication, approved/failed-label conflicts, graph-construction caps, splitting, and exact-leakage checks. In the prepared DRKG map, 1,130/1,571 repoDB compounds match (1,106/1,519 among approved compounds), whereas none of the 2,051 disease CUIs matches the tested literal forms. Local graph-ID coverage is therefore distinguished from cross-resource alignment. We reproduced normalization, splitting, and all 90,804 evidence-graph edges from the repository functions and confirmed the 5,677/500/500 approved split with zero held-out target triples in the evidence graph.

Revisions: Section 4.1 points to the new Supplementary Section S11.1; Table S15 clarifies the meaning of entity coverage.



Comment 6. The authors should cite and discuss the following two relevant works to contextualize the proposed method against advanced graph learning paradigms for drug entities, and discuss how more principled multi-source fusion approaches could advance beyond heuristic hand-calibrated evidence scoring: "Spatial-spectral fusion enables drug repositioning by capturing indirect and long-range associations in biological networks",

"FD-MSGL: Drug Repositioning via Frequency-Domain Multi-Source Synergistic Graph Learning".

Author response:

We thank the reviewer for these relevant suggestions. Both requested studies are cited and discussed in Section 2.1: spatial–spectral fusion for indirect and long-range associations, and FD-MSGL for frequency-domain multi-source graph learning.

Section 5 now explains how source-specific molecular, interaction, and pathway representations could be learned jointly with fusion weights and a ranking objective. Coupling that objective to a graph budget and path-retention criteria could guide evidence selection, while source-wise gating could accommodate missing or conflicting modalities. We distinguish this proposed extension from the shared linear calibration actually evaluated here; the present experiments do not implement either cited architecture.

Revisions: Section 2.1, References 45–46, and the final paragraph of Section 5.
