\subsection{Frozen-model representation and generated-answer results}

The completed GPU study comprises 28 variants on each of five datasets,
yielding 140 summaries and 58,800 row--variant evaluations of the same 2,100
test rows. Every comparison uses the same dataset-specific checkpoint and
fixed candidate order. The precision policy is FP32 for graph encoding and
BF16 for the LLM throughout; no old FP16 predictions are substituted.

\paragraph{Structural loss and output invariance are distinct.}
On Hetionet, original fuzzy retrieval preserves the candidate encoder outputs
(mean cosine 1.0000, relative L2 0.0000) and all generated answers relative to
the source graph. Nevertheless, query outputs change (mean cosine 0.9209,
relative L2 0.3259), and all eligible length-three path witnesses are lost.
Thus even exact candidate-output agreement cannot be interpreted as whole-graph
information preservation. Directed message passing, global embeddings, and
which nodes are injected into the prompt all affect how a structural change
propagates to generation.

DRKG likewise has 100\% generated-answer agreement for fuzzy versus source,
although mean query and candidate cosines are 0.9312 and 0.9867; their relative
L2 changes are 0.3834 and 0.0526. On PrimeKG and PharmKG, answer agreement is only 78.8\% and
80.0\%, respectively. Their candidate cosines are 0.9453 and 0.9991, while
query cosines are lower at 0.8828 and 0.8718. These results show why candidate
similarity alone is insufficient to characterize information supplied to the
LLM.

On repoDB, fuzzy/source answer agreement is 80.8\%, with query/candidate
cosines of 0.9814/0.9806. Fuzzy generates 2/500 exact answers, compared with
1/500 for source raw, 11/500 for native-score top-$k$, and 9/500 for the
empty-local-graph reference. The latter retains frozen global embeddings;
it is not a knowledge-free model. These counterexamples are reported without
selecting a new method using test outcomes.

\paragraph{Downstream differences do not establish a universal winner.}
The exact-accuracy comparison does not show consistent superiority of fuzzy
over all alternative selectors. Several differences correspond to only one
correct answer in a 500-row test set. For example, fuzzy produces 0/500,
1/500, 3/100, and 7/500 correct answers on PrimeKG, PharmKG, Hetionet, and
DRKG, respectively. The joint query/seed and paired query bootstrap tables
are supplied alongside these point estimates; lack of a detected difference
is not a demonstration of equivalence. These low counts limit conclusions
about small downstream improvements.

Adjusted MRR is intentionally kept separate from exact accuracy. It assigns
credit based on the pre-existing candidate order after a predicted candidate
is promoted to the front, with zero credit when the gold is absent from the
candidate set. It can remain positive when no generated answer is correct.
Under the strict normalized matching rule, 59.0\% of PharmKG fuzzy outputs
do not match a listed candidate. This is an output-format/matching diagnostic,
not evidence that those outputs are valid biological alternatives. The full
workbook includes invalid-output and top-candidate-copy rates for every variant.

\paragraph{Numerical and computational scope.}
The FP16 audit failure is localized to nine PrimeKG source-graph cases among
the 4,200 source/fuzzy cases checked; the paired FP32 audit contains no
nonfinite outputs. The maximum finite FP32 projection magnitude is
130,444.35, exceeding the FP16 maximum of 65,504, motivating the BF16 LLM
input policy as well as FP32 graph computation. Final inference checks
finiteness before and after casting. All test rows remain included.

The accompanying benchmark reports graph-encoder call timing and resident
plus peak allocated CUDA memory, with the LLM already resident but generation
excluded. It does not establish an end-to-end LLM speedup. The full predictions,
input hashes, model fingerprints, seed results, and completion markers are
retained for audit and reproducibility.
